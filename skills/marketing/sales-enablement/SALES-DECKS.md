# Sales decks

Slide-by-slide guidance for a sales deck that tells a story and closes deals. All of it is from coreyhaines31/marketingskills, skill `sales-enablement` (SKILL.md and references/deck-frameworks.md, MIT), kept close to the source's wording. The deliverable is a slide-by-slide outline with headline, body copy and speaker notes.

## The 11-slide framework

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, sales-enablement] The source's heading says "10-12 slides" and lists 11:

1. **Current World Problem:** the pain your buyer lives with today
2. **Cost of the Problem:** what inaction costs (time, money, risk)
3. **The Shift Happening:** market or technology change creating urgency
4. **Your Approach:** how you solve it differently
5. **Product Walkthrough:** 3-4 key workflows, not a feature tour
6. **Proof Points:** metrics, logos, analyst recognition
7. **Case Study:** one customer story told well
8. **Implementation / Timeline:** how they get from here to live
9. **ROI / Value:** expected return and payback period
10. **Pricing Overview:** transparent, tiered if applicable
11. **Next Steps / CTA:** clear action with timeline

Deck principles:

- **Story arc, not feature tour.** Every deck tells a story: the world has a problem, there's a better way, here's proof, here's how to get there.
- **One idea per slide.** If you need two points, use two slides.
- **Design for presenting, not reading.** Slides support the conversation; they don't replace it. Minimal text, strong visuals.

## The storytelling arc

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, sales-enablement] Situation → Complication → Resolution.

- **Situation** (slides 1-3): the world your buyer lives in. Establish shared understanding.
- **Complication** (slides 2-3): why the status quo is no longer sustainable. Create urgency.
- **Resolution** (slides 4-11): your approach, proof, and path forward.

The ranges overlap in the source (slides 2-3 are both Situation and Complication); they are copied as written. "The goal is not to present features. The goal is to make the buyer feel understood, then show them a better way."

## Slide by slide

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, sales-enablement]

| Slide | Include | Avoid | Copy prompt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Current World Problem | The challenge your buyer faces daily; a stat or data point that quantifies the problem; visual: simple graphic or striking number | Starting with your company or product; generic industry trends that don't connect to pain; more than one core problem | "What is the one problem that, if you could describe it perfectly, would make your buyer say 'that's exactly my situation'?" |
| 2 Cost of the Problem | Financial impact (revenue lost, costs incurred); time impact (hours wasted, delays); risk impact (what happens if they do nothing); specific numbers wherever possible | Vague claims without data; fear-mongering without substance; too many metrics (pick 2-3 that hit hardest) | "If your buyer does nothing for the next 12 months, what does it cost them?" |
| 3 The Shift Happening | Market trend or technology change creating a new opportunity; why "the old way" no longer works; why now is the right time to act | Hype-driven trends without substance; making it about your product yet; overly technical explanations | "What has changed in the market that makes the old approach unsustainable?" |
| 4 Your Approach | Your philosophy or unique point of view; how your approach differs from conventional solutions; the "aha" insight that led to your product | Feature lists (too early); jargon or acronyms; claiming to be "the only" or "the first" unless provably true | "What do you believe about solving this problem that most people get wrong?" |
| 5 Product Walkthrough | 3-4 key workflows that map to the pain from slide 1; screenshots or product visuals; brief description of what each workflow accomplishes | Showing every feature; dense UI screenshots without callouts; talking about technology instead of outcomes | "Walk through 3 things the buyer would do in your product in their first week." |
| 6 Proof Points | Customer logos (aim for recognizable names in their industry); key metrics: "X% improvement," "Y hours saved," "Z% increase"; analyst recognition, awards, or certifications if relevant | Unsubstantiated claims; too many logos without context; vanity metrics that don't relate to the buyer's pain | "What are 3 numbers that prove your product works?" |
| 7 Case Study | One customer story told well: challenge, solution, results; specific metrics (before and after); customer quote if available; a customer similar to the prospect | Multiple case studies crammed into one slide; generic outcomes without specifics; customers from irrelevant industries | "Tell the story of one customer who went from struggling to succeeding with your product." |
| 8 Implementation / Timeline | Clear phases with timeline (e.g., Week 1: Setup, Week 2-3: Integration, Week 4: Live); what's required from their side vs. yours; support resources available | Overcomplicating the process; hiding time requirements; skipping the "what do I need to do?" question | "How does a customer get from signing to live? What does each week look like?" |
| 9 ROI / Value | Expected return based on their inputs or industry benchmarks; payback period; total value over 1-3 years; comparison to cost of inaction | Unrealistic projections; ROI without showing your math; generic numbers not tied to their situation | "If they buy today, what does the next 12 months look like in dollars and hours?" |
| 10 Pricing Overview | Pricing tiers or structure; what's included at each level; recommended plan for their situation | Burying the price or being cagey; too many options (3 tiers max); surprising them with hidden costs | "What does it cost, what do they get, and which plan is right for them?" |
| 11 Next Steps / CTA | Specific next action with timeline ("Start a pilot next week"); what happens after they say yes; your contact information | Vague CTAs ("Let's stay in touch"); multiple competing next steps; ending without energy | "What is the one thing you want them to do after this meeting?" |

Every logo, metric, award and certification on slides 6, 7 and 9 is one the company really has, with the customer's agreement to be shown; results claims follow [SALES-ASSETS.md](SALES-ASSETS.md#case-study-briefs-sales-format).

## Emphasis by buyer

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, sales-enablement]

| Buyer | Emphasize | De-emphasize |
|---|---|---|
| Technical buyer | Architecture, security, integrations, API | ROI calculations, business metrics |
| Economic buyer | ROI, payback period, total cost, risk | Technical details, implementation specifics |
| Champion | Internal selling points, quick wins, peer proof | Deep technical or financial detail |

### Technical buyer deck

- **Add:** an architecture diagram slide after Product Walkthrough; security and compliance details; integration ecosystem and API capabilities; technical implementation requirements.
- **Remove or minimize:** ROI calculations (they care about capability, not cost); high-level market trends (they want specifics).
- **Tone:** precise, no fluff, respect their expertise. Avoid marketing superlatives.

### Economic buyer deck

- **Add:** a detailed ROI slide with calculations shown; total cost of ownership comparison; risk mitigation and compliance; an executive summary slide up front.
- **Remove or minimize:** technical details and architecture; feature-level walkthroughs; implementation specifics (they'll delegate).
- **Tone:** business-focused, outcome-driven. Speak in dollars and percentages.

### Champion deck

- **Add:** an "internal selling" slide (key points for them to present to their team); a quick-win slide (what success looks like in 30 days); peer proof (companies like theirs who succeeded); objection pre-handling (common pushback they'll face internally).
- **Remove or minimize:** deep technical or financial detail; anything that requires context they can't relay.
- **Tone:** empowering, equipping. "Make them look smart to their boss."

## Anti-patterns

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, sales-enablement]

- **The Feature Dump:** every slide is a feature with a screenshot. No story, no "so what," no connection to the buyer's world. Reps click through it; prospects tune out.
- **The Wall of Text:** slides with 200+ words. Nobody reads them during a presentation. If the slide requires reading, it belongs in a leave-behind ([ONE-PAGERS.md](ONE-PAGERS.md)).
- **The Missing Story Arc:** slides exist in isolation, with no narrative flow from problem to solution to proof. The deck feels like a brochure, not a conversation.
- **The Generic Screenshot:** product screenshots without callouts, annotations, or context. The prospect can't tell what they're looking at or why it matters.
- **The Premature Demo:** jumping to product features before establishing the problem. The buyer has no frame of reference for why your features matter.
- **The Kitchen Sink:** trying to address every persona, every use case, every feature in one deck. The result is a "40-slide monster" that nobody wants to sit through.

## Prove it

The outline has one idea per slide with headline, body and speaker notes; slide 1 names one problem and doesn't open with the company; slide 2 uses 2-3 metrics; no "the only" or "the first" without proof; pricing shows at most 3 tiers and no hidden costs; slide 11 has one next step with a date; the variant matches the buyer (architecture slide for technical, executive summary and shown ROI math for economic, internal-selling and 30-day quick-win slides for a champion); no slide carries 200+ words.
