# Which events to invest in

The layer above role and tactics, copied from coreyhaines31/marketingskills `events` (MIT), `references/event-portfolio-strategy.md`, "Distilled from Corey Haines's *Founding Marketing*". The leverage is in "selection and portfolio design, not execution". Every company result below is the book's anecdote, and the cost table is the source's directional range: "re-verify current show pricing."

## Is in-person necessary

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, events] "Digital scales efficiently; in-person builds trust that digital can't." Prioritize events when the ICP is:

- **Enterprise or multi-stakeholder:** high ACV, several people must trust you before a big commitment.
- **Regulated buyers:** healthcare, finance, government, with strict vendor-evaluation norms.
- **High-touch or heavily customized:** significant integration or configuration work.
- **Conservative industries:** manufacturing, utilities.
- **Long cycles:** 6+ month sales cycles.

The book's example: a cybersecurity company whose $500k+ ACV deals "almost never closed without at least one in-person meeting". If the ICP is in none of these buckets, "spend on digital first and treat events as a small experiment."

## The 80/20 of events

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, events] A few events generate most event pipeline (the book's example: "3 conferences drove ~70%" for one B2B SaaS program).

- Increase presence at the winners: speaking slots, larger side events, more of the right people, better placement.
- Cut or minimize the long tail.
- Re-rank yearly; the winners shift as your ICP and market move.
- "Each event is its own ecosystem": never write off the channel after one bad conference.

## Bigger is often worse

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, events]

- **Big events mean more noise:** higher cost on everything (booth, hotels, travel), more competing vendors, attendees spread across tracks, endless competing side events.
- **Audience dilution:** the crowd is padded with students, investors, press, other vendors, consultants and "industry tourists", so cost per qualified lead balloons.
- **Small events win:** "highest ROI is often regional events of 100-200 where you can reach every qualified prospect in the room"; the source's main file puts the range at 50 to 200 attendees.

## Three event types

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, events]

**Owned events: maximum control, maximum risk.** From exec dinners to roadshows, summits and user conferences.

- User conferences turn customers into a community; don't attempt one "before you have an audience that would come unbegged."
- Regional roadshows take the message to scattered markets (the book: a 6-city roadshow out-produced an annual conference "at a third of the cost").
- Industry summits tackle category problems, not product pitches, and pull in partners who amplify.
- Workshops and certifications tie the event to customer success and can pay for themselves through fees.
- Three success factors: ruthless audience focus (a clear "who", even at the expense of broader appeal); a value proposition attendees can't get elsewhere; timing aligned to buyer budget and bandwidth (the book: moving a conference from Q4 to Q1 lifted attendance 40%).
- The book's model case, Drift HYPERGROWTH, dropped badges and sponsor booths and chose storytelling over product pitches.

**Trade shows and conferences: someone else's arena.** "Success is 120 days of prep, not the 4 days on the floor."

- **Pre-show, from about 120 days out:** mine the attendee list for stories (recent funding, press, job posts) rather than names, and book about 70% of meeting slots before anyone flies. The hook references their news and an outcome you delivered for similar companies, with a coffee at the show as the ask.
- **On the floor:** make the booth "a story-collection hub": senior staff at the edges, not behind a counter; no physical barriers; customer stories on screens; real customers telling their story.
- **Satellite events:** morning coffee meetups and curated private dinners "routinely out-generate the booth" ([SPONSORSHIP.md](SPONSORSHIP.md#the-side-event)).
- **Post-show:** tier leads and reference specific conversation details: hot the same day, warm personalized within 48h, general into nurture within a week; turn booth conversations into content.

**Community events: compound interest.** Small, regular, often on a tiny budget.

- "Regular rhythm beats flash": same format, same venue, every month.
- "Never pitch, facilitate."
- Support customer-run user groups but let them stay independent: prospects trust them "*because* they're not on your payroll".
- Arm your most engaged attendees with playbooks, speaker connections and seed funding to launch their own city events.
- Metrics: monthly active members, conversation depth, community-initiated events, relationship velocity, member-to-customer conversion. The gut check is the **Saturday Test**: "would people show up on a Saturday morning?"
- The book claims prospects who attended 3+ community events showed "85% higher close rate and 40% shorter cycle"; an anecdote, not a benchmark. Running the community itself is [community-marketing COMMUNITY.md](https://github.com/Quegenx/agent-skills/blob/main/skills/marketing/community-marketing/COMMUNITY.md).

## Costs

[coreyhaines31/marketingskills, events] Budget money and time against pipeline, not the sticker price. The source's directional ranges:

| Line item | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Conference ticket | $1,500 to 3,000 per person (major shows) |
| Booth space (10x10, top-tier) | $15,000 to 40,000 |
| Flights | $300 to 1,000 per person |
| Hotel | $300 to 400 per night per person |
| Booth staff | 3 to 4 people minimum at any significant show |
| Private dinner (15 to 20 people) | $150 to 200 per person |
| Breakfast meetup | $30 to 50 per person |
| Happy hour | $50 per person |
| Private meeting room | $500 to 1,500 per day |

Rule of thumb: "a significant show needs to generate ~5-10 solid opportunities to justify sending a team." The sponsor go or no-go math is [SPONSORSHIP.md](SPONSORSHIP.md#should-we-sponsor).

## Prove it

- Each event on the calendar has a stated reason in-person fits the ICP, or is marked as a small experiment.
- Last year's events are ranked by pipeline, with the top ones getting more presence and the tail cut.
- Each event's budget totals tickets, booth, travel, hotels and staff time with current prices, and names the opportunities it must produce.
- Book figures used in a plan are labelled as the book's anecdotes.
